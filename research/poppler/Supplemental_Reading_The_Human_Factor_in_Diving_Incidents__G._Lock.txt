So What? What Can I Do?

As I have pointed out, we all make mistakes, irrespective of
our experience and knowledge. By providing feedback into the
system, we are able to improve the overall safety of diving—in
effect, to learn from others’ mistakes and experiences. We should
recognize that “near-miss” incidents provide more opportunity to
identify failures than looking at just fatalities. One of the most
obvious reasons for this is that the divers are able to explain why
they made the decisions they did considering their experience,
and importantly, the context in which the decisions were made.

Consider the following example of a very experienced OC diver
and instructor and fairly new KISS CCR diver. The diver used to
forget to the turn off the CCR O2 valve after they had finished a
dive. (The KISS is a constant mass flow mCCR and this meant
the O2 cylinder was empty the following day). The diver then
got into the (good) habit of turning the unit off when sitting on
the bench of the boat as part of his de-kitting procedures. This
gradually progressed to turning the unit off whilst standing on
the diver lift. On the day in question, the diver was waiting for
another diver to get onto the diver lift and they would be next.
Thinking they would be back on the boat in a couple of minutes,
they turned off the O2. Unfortunately the boat went around and
